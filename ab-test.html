Experiment brief · Prototype

Segment-first homepage

Replace the search-led entry point with three choices, and route each one into a flow built for it. Two candidate cuts: by need (Klein · Familie · Sport) and by drivetrain (Elektro · Hybrid · Benzin). Both are built — the test decides which, if either, beats search.

The problem

A search box assumes the visitor already knows what they want. For a subscription product that is rarely true: people arrive knowing why they need a car — a second child, a new commute, a lease that just ended — not which car. So they land in a 70-model grid with filters for fuel type, gearbox and body style, and are asked to translate a life situation into car-industry vocabulary themselves.

That translation step is where undecided traffic leaks. It also costs us the single most valuable thing a first click can give us: a declared intent.

Hypothesis

If we replace search with three need-based entry points, then homepage → configurator conversion increases, because visitors choose from a decision they can actually make (why) instead of one they can't yet (which).

Why exactly these three

Three is the point where the choice is still instant. Each one maps to a different buying psychology, and therefore to a different flow — that's the real substance of the test, not the cards themselves.

Two ways to cut the same traffic

B and C differ in one thing only: what the first click is about. Everything after it — the PDP, the checkout — is untouched in both.

If we run all three arms at once, the sample per arm drops and the test takes roughly 50 % longer. My recommendation: run A vs B first, because B addresses the undecided visitor — the group search already fails. Hold C as the follow-up, or ship it as a secondary entry point below the fold where it costs nothing to measure.

This isn't a copy change, so I'd run it as one experiment with a shared primary metric rather than three separate tests — otherwise we can't attribute the effect to the concept.

Sizing

Two-sided test, α = 0.05, power = 80 %, primary baseline 6 %, minimum detectable effect +10 % relative:

≈ 25,000 sessions per variant → ~50,000 total → 7–10 days at an assumed 6–8k homepage sessions/day.

Run full weeks to avoid weekday bias. No peeking — one read at the end, and I'd hold the call if the guardrails and the primary metric disagree.

Risks and how I'd handle them

RiskMitigation
Three buckets don't cover everyone (vans, business, EV-only shoppers)Keep the escape hatch and the full model grid on the page. If escape-hatch usage is high, that's a finding, not a failure — it sizes the fourth segment.
"Small" is read as size, not value — a cheap compact SUV belongs there tooNaming is its own follow-up test. Label copy is the cheapest thing to iterate once the concept is validated.
Quiz adds friction for the family segmentCut to two questions, one tap each, auto-advance, back button, progress bar. We never ask for a budget — asking it invites the customer to anchor low before we have made the safety argument.
SEO impact from changing the homepage entryModel and category pages stay untouched and stay linked; only the hero module is swapped.
CTR goes up, conversion goes downThat's the guardrail's job. Ship decisions come from the guardrails, not the headline number.

If it wins

  1. The segment becomes a first-class signal. It's captured on the first click and carried through email, retargeting and returning-visitor personalisation — worth more than the CTR lift itself.
  2. Family gets the real investment. Highest anxiety, longest consideration, biggest upsell headroom — and the three-tier screen is where the mix shifts.
  3. Round two: test the naming, test four segments vs three, test whether Sport should hide price above the fold entirely, and test the middle tier's price gap — the decoy only works while the ladder stays credible.

About this prototype

Hand-built as static HTML with vanilla JavaScript — no framework, no build step, no backend. It runs from a folder by opening index.html.

The 102 vehicles, their prices, term and mileage packages, equipment lists and photos are real data pulled from finn.com, so the recommendation logic and the upsell maths compute on actual numbers rather than placeholders. The scoring model, quiz weighting, three-tier anchoring, price-per-term selector, mileage upsell and the €/100 km comparison all compute on that data.

Open the browser console to watch the tracking events fire (segment_selected, quiz_answer, tier_click, checkout_start) — that's the instrumentation the experiment would need.

Built with Claude Code in a single working session. It is a discussion prop, not a FINN product; all imagery and vehicle data belong to FINN.
